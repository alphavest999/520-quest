# BP795

Researchers investigated the acid‑base behavior of five common amino acids—glycine, alanine, aspartic acid, lysine, and tryptophan—by performing titration experiments at 25 °C. For each amino acid, a 0.01 M solution was prepared in deionized water. A standardized 0.1 M HCl solution was added in 0.5 mL increments while the pH was recorded after each addition. The initial pH of each solution, as well as the known pKa values for the carboxyl (COOH) and amino (NH3⁺) functional groups, are summarized in Table 1. The resulting titration curves displayed the expected buffering regions near each pKa. The data allow calculation of the isoelectric point (pI) for each amino acid, which is the pH at which the net charge of the molecule is zero. These experiments illustrate how the side‑chain chemistry of an amino acid influences its overall acid‑base properties.

| Glycine    | 8.3        | 2.34       | 9.60       |
| Alanine    | 8.4        | 2.35       | 9.70       |
| Aspartic Acid | 5.1     | 1.88       | 9.60       |
| Lysine     | 9.5        | 2.18       | 10.53      |
| Tryptophan | 8.8        | 1.92       | 9.30       |

## 1 Which amino acid has the highest initial pH in the experiment?
Correct: C
10‑yr‑old analogy: Imagine the pH scale as a thermometer for acidity. The higher the number, the less acidic (more basic) the solution. Lysine starts at the highest pH, so it is the most basic among the five. Official: Lysine’s initial pH is 9.5, higher than the others. Wrong answer: Glycine’s initial pH is 8.3, lower than lysine’s.

## 2 If the concentration of the amino‑acid solution were increased from 0.01 M to 0.05 M, what would happen to the slope of the titration curve?
Correct: B
10‑yr‑old analogy: Think of the curve as a hill. A thicker solution is like a wider hill that rises more gently. Official: Higher concentration increases buffering capacity, making the slope less steep. Wrong answer: A steeper slope would mean less buffering, which is the opposite of what occurs.

## 3 Calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of glycine using the pKa values provided.
Correct: A
10‑yr‑old analogy: The pI is like the middle of a seesaw between two numbers. For glycine, the middle of 2.34 and 9.60 is about 5.97. Official: pI = (pKa1 + pKa2)/2 = (2.34+9.60)/2 = 5.97. Wrong answer: 7.97 would be the middle of 5.97 and 9.97, which is not relevant.

## 4 Which unit is incorrectly used when converting moles of HCl added to moles of H⁺?
Correct: D
10‑yr‑old analogy: Think of moles like counting apples. You count apples, not apples per kilogram of soil. Official: Moles are a count, not a mass‑based unit. Wrong answer: mol/kg would incorrectly tie the count to mass.

## 5 Which concept is directly related to the buffering capacity observed in the titration curves?
Correct: A
10‑yr‑old analogy: The buffering capacity is like a sponge that absorbs acid. The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation tells us how much acid a buffer can hold. Official: Buffering is governed by the Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship. Wrong answer: Michaelis–Menten deals with enzyme kinetics, not buffering.
