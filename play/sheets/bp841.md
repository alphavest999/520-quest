# BP841

Researchers at the University of Biochemistry conducted a systematic titration study on four common amino acids—glycine, alanine, lysine, and aspartic acid—to determine their acid dissociation constants (pKa) and isoelectric points (pI). Using a calibrated pH meter, they added incremental aliquots of 0.1 M NaOH to 10 mL solutions of each amino acid at 25 °C, recording the pH after each addition. The titration curves revealed distinct inflection points corresponding to the protonation/deprotonation of the amino, carboxyl, and side‑chain functional groups. The researchers tabulated the observed pKa values and calculated the pI as the average of the two pKa values surrounding the net zero charge. The data were then plotted to visualize how each amino acid’s charge changes with pH. This experiment provides a clear illustration of how structural differences influence pKa and pI, essential concepts for understanding protein behavior in different environments.

| Amino Acid | pKa1 (COOH) | pKa2 (NH3+) | pI |
|------------|-------------|-------------|-----|
| Glycine    | 2.34        | 9.60        | 5.97 |
| Alanine    | 2.35        | 9.70        | 6.02 |
| Lysine     | 2.18        | 9.06        | 9.62 |
| Aspartic Acid | 1.88    | 3.65        | 2.77 |


## bp841-01 Which amino acid has the highest isoelectric point (pI) according to the data?
Correct: C
Analogy 10yr: Think of a pI as the "balance point" where a ball on a seesaw has no tilt. The ball that stays level at the highest point on the seesaw is Lysine. Official: pI is calculated as the average of the two pKa values that bracket a net zero charge; Lysine’s pKa1 and pKa2 average to 9.62, the highest among the four. Why wrong: Choosing Glycine or Alanine assumes the highest pKa1 gives the highest pI, but pI depends on both pKa values, not just the first.

## bp841-02 If the researchers had used 0.1 M HCl instead of NaOH for titration, what would be the primary effect on the titration curves?
Correct: A
Analogy 10yr: Imagine a slide that starts high; adding acid pushes the slide down faster. Official: Using HCl adds protons, so the solution stays more acidic, shifting the pH vs. volume curve leftward. Why wrong: Thinking the curves shift right assumes a base is added, but HCl does the opposite.

## bp841-03 Calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of aspartic acid given its pKa values: pKa1 = 1.88, pKa2 = 3.65, pKa3 = 9.60.
Correct: A
Analogy 10yr: Picture two stops on a train track; the middle stop is the balance point. For aspartic acid, the middle stop is between pKa2 (3.65) and pKa3 (9.60). Official: pI = (3.65 + 9.60)/2 = 6.625 ≈ 6.62. Why wrong: Picking 6.90 or 7.62 assumes the wrong pair of pKa values, ignoring that the carboxyl side chain is already deprotonated at neutral pH.

## bp841-04 Which of the following units is incorrectly used for concentration in this experiment?
Correct: D
Analogy 10yr: Think of measuring water with a cup; using a cup that’s too small (cm³) would give a wrong amount. Official: In aqueous chemistry, concentration is expressed in mol/L or mol/dm³ (equivalent). mol/cm³ is a volume unit of 1 cm³ = 1 mL, leading to a concentration 1000 times too high. Why wrong: Some students confuse cm³ with L, but 1 L = 1000 cm³, not 1.
