# BP817

Researchers performed potentiometric titration of five amino acids—glycine, alanine, aspartic acid, lysine, and phenylalanine—in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at 25 °C, recording pH changes upon addition of standardized 0.1 M NaOH. The resulting titration curves were analyzed to determine the pKa values for the carboxyl, amino, and side‑chain functional groups. The data were tabulated and used to calculate the isoelectric points (pI) for each amino acid. The experiment included careful control of ionic strength and temperature to minimize systematic error. The titration data were plotted as pH versus moles of NaOH added, and the inflection points were identified by the first derivative method. The resulting pKa values were compared to literature values to validate the experimental approach.

## bp817-001 Which amino acid has the highest pKa1 value according to the experimental data?
Correct: B
Analogy for a 10‑year‑old: Imagine each amino acid has a special “pKa” number that tells how easily it loses a proton from its carboxyl group. The higher the number, the harder it is to lose that proton. Among the five amino acids, Alanine’s number is the highest, so it’s the hardest to lose a proton from its carboxyl group. Official explanation: pKa1 values are determined by the carboxyl group; the experimental data show Alanine’s pKa1 (2.35) is greater than the others. Why the other choices are wrong: Glycine (2.34) and Lysine (2.18) have lower pKa1 values, and Aspartic Acid (1.88) is even lower.

## bp817-002 If the researchers performed the titration at 35 °C instead of 25 °C, how would the measured pKa values change?
Correct: B
Analogy for a 10‑year‑old: Think of temperature as a heat source that makes acids act a bit softer. When it gets warmer, acids give up their protons more easily, so the pKa number (which tells how hard it is to lose a proton) goes down. Official explanation: Increasing temperature generally lowers pKa values for most acids. Why the other choices are wrong: The pKa values do not increase or stay the same; they shift downward.

## bp817-003 Calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of lysine using the provided pKa values.
Correct: A
Analogy for a 10‑year‑old: The isoelectric point is like the balance point where the positive and negative charges on lysine cancel each other out. To find it, you average the two middle pKa numbers (the ones for the two NH3⁺ groups). For lysine, (8.95 + 10.53)/2 = 9.74. Official explanation: pI = (pKa2 + pKa3)/2 for basic amino acids. Why the other choices are wrong: 8.95 is just one of the pKa values, 10.53 is the other, and 7.00 is not related.

## bp817-004 Which unit is incorrectly used in the titration data?
Correct: A
Analogy for a 10‑year‑old: When scientists talk about how much of a substance is in a solution, they use a unit that tells how many grams or moles are in a certain volume—this is called molarity. Saying just “moles” is like saying you have 5 apples without telling how many apples per basket. Official explanation: Concentration units used in titration are molarity (mol L⁻¹). Moles, molality, and mole fraction are not concentration units. Why the other choices are wrong: They are valid concentration units; only moles alone is incorrect.
