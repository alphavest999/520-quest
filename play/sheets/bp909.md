# BP909

Researchers at the Institute of Biochemistry conducted a systematic titration study to determine the acid–base dissociation constants (pKa) of a representative set of twenty standard amino acids. Using a UV–visible spectrophotometer, they recorded absorbance at 280 nm while incrementally adjusting the solution pH from 1.0 to 13.0 with a calibrated pH meter. The resulting titration curves displayed distinct inflection points corresponding to the ionization of the α‑carboxyl, α‑amino, and, where present, side‑chain functional groups. By applying the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation to the slope of each linear segment, the researchers extracted precise pKa values. The data were summarized in Table 1, which lists the pKa of the carboxyl, amino, and side‑chain groups for each amino acid. These values were subsequently used to calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of each residue, a key parameter in protein purification and electrophoresis. The experiment demonstrates how systematic variation of a single independent variable—pH—enables the determination of multiple dependent variables (pKa values) that define the acid–base behavior of amino acids.

## bp909-1 Which variable did the researchers systematically alter to determine the pKa values of the amino acids?
Correct: B
Imagine you have a toy car that you want to see how fast it goes. You decide to change the speed of the car by turning the steering wheel. The steering wheel is the independent variable because you are the one changing it, while the car's speed is what you measure. In the experiment, the researchers changed the pH of the solution; that is the independent variable. The pKa values are what they measured (dependent). Some might mistakenly think the pKa is the variable you change, but it's actually what you observe.

## bp909-2 If the researchers had kept the temperature constant but varied the ionic strength of the solution, what would be the effect on the measured pKa values?
Correct: C
Imagine you have a recipe that you keep the oven at the same temperature but add more salt. The taste of the cake doesn’t change much because the baking temperature is what controls the rise. Similarly, pKa values are largely independent of ionic strength under the conditions used, so changing ionic strength would not shift the pKa. Some might think ionic strength affects pKa, but the experiment shows temperature is the key factor.

## bp909-3 Using the table, calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of lysine.
Correct: B
To find the isoelectric point of lysine, you take the average of the two pKa values that bracket the zero net charge: the α‑amino (9.74) and the side‑chain amino (10.53). (9.74+10.53)/2 = 10.135, rounded to 10.13. Some might average all three pKa values or use the wrong pair, leading to a wrong answer.

## bp909-4 Which of the following statements is incorrect regarding the determination of pI from pKa values?
Correct: D
The statement that the pI of lysine is higher than that of arginine is incorrect because arginine's side‑chain pKa (12.48) makes its pI higher (≈11.55) than lysine's (≈10.13). Some might think that because lysine has two basic groups, its pI is higher, but the side‑chain pKa of arginine is higher. The trap is confusing the number of basic groups with the magnitude of pKa.

## bp909-5 Which of the following best describes the relationship between the pKa values listed in the table and the pI of an amino acid?
Correct: C
The pI is calculated as the average of the pKa values of the groups that change charge around the point where the net charge is zero. It is not simply the lowest or highest pKa. The correct statement is that pI is the average of the two pKa values that bracket zero charge.
