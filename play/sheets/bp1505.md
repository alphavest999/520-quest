# BP1505

Researchers investigated how varying light intensity affects the rate of photosynthetic oxygen production in the model plant *Arabidopsis thaliana*. Leaf discs (2 mm diameter) were exposed to six discrete light intensities (200, 400, 600, 800, 1,000, and 1,200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) using a calibrated LED array. Oxygen evolution was measured in a Clark-type electrode over a 10‑minute period, and the mean O₂ production rate (µmol O₂ g⁻¹ FW h⁻¹) was recorded for each intensity. The data revealed a classic light‑response curve: rates increased sharply up to 800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, plateaued between 800–1,000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and declined slightly at 1,200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, suggesting photoinhibition. The authors concluded that optimal photosynthetic performance occurs around 800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for this cultivar under the experimental conditions. The study underscores the importance of matching light supply to plant capacity to avoid energy waste and potential damage.

Correct: C
The table shows that the O₂ production rate peaks at 1,000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (60.2 µmol O₂ g⁻¹ FW h⁻¹). Rates at 800 and 1,200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ are lower, and the lowest intensity gives the smallest rate.

## bp1505_q2 Which of the following is the most appropriate control for this photosynthesis experiment?
Correct: A
A dark control establishes the baseline (no photosynthetic O₂ evolution) and confirms that the observed oxygen production is light‑dependent. The other options either provide light (B), alter the measurement apparatus (C), or chemically inhibit photosynthesis (D), which do not serve as a baseline control.

## bp1505_q3 Calculate the approximate slope (µmol O₂ g⁻¹ FW h⁻¹ per µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) of the linear portion of the light‑response curve (between 200 and 800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹).
Correct: B
Linear slope = (58.9 – 12.3) / (800 – 200) = 46.6 / 600 ≈ 0.0776, which rounds to 0.10 µmol O₂ g⁻¹ FW h⁻¹ per µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

## bp1505_q4 Which of the following is NOT a potential source of error in this experiment?
Correct: D
The Clark electrode in this study is specified to detect O₂. Using a CO₂‑sensitive electrode would render the data meaningless, but it is not a plausible error because the equipment was chosen for O₂ detection. The other options represent realistic experimental errors.
