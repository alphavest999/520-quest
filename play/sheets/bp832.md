# BP832

Researchers investigated the titration of four common amino acids—glycine, L-arginine, L-lysine, and L-phenylalanine—to determine their acid–base dissociation constants (pKa) and isoelectric points (pI). Each amino acid solution (0.1 M) was titrated with 0.1 M HCl while monitoring pH with a calibrated glass electrode. The volume of acid added at each inflection point was recorded, allowing calculation of pKa values using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. The experiment was performed at 25 °C, and the pH scale was calibrated with standard buffers (pH 4.00, 7.00, and 10.00). The data were plotted as pH versus volume of acid added, revealing clear sigmoidal curves for each amino acid. The researchers then used the midpoint of each transition to derive the pKa values and calculated the pI as the average of the two pKa values for the α‑COOH and α‑NH3+ groups. The results were compared to literature values to validate the methodology.

| Amino Acid | pKa (α‑COOH) | pKa (α‑NH3+) | pI |
|------------|--------------|--------------|-----|
| Glycine    | 2.34         | 9.60         | 5.97 |
| L‑Arginine | 2.17         | 9.04         | 10.61 |
| L‑Lysine   | 2.18         | 9.06         | 10.62 |
| L‑Phenylalanine | 1.83 | 9.24 | 5.54 |

## bp832-1 Which variable did the researchers manipulate to determine the pKa values of the amino acids?
Correct: C
Analogy 10‑yr‑old: Imagine you’re filling a balloon with water to see how big it gets. You’re changing the amount of water you pour in. Official: The researchers varied the amount of strong acid (HCl) added; that’s the independent variable. Why wrong: Thinking the concentration or temperature was varied would mean you’re changing the starting conditions, not the titrant volume.

## bp832-2 If the researchers had used a phosphate buffer instead of 0.1 M HCl during titration, what would be the most likely effect on the observed pKa values?
Correct: A
Analogy 10‑yr‑old: Think of trying to measure how fast a toy car slows down on a track that’s already sticky. The sticky track (buffer) changes the car’s speed. Official: A buffer partially neutralizes the added acid, so the titration curve shifts, giving lower apparent pKa values. Why wrong: Assuming no change ignores the buffer’s capacity to resist pH change.

## bp832-3 Using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of glycine.
Correct: A
Analogy 10‑yr‑old: If you have two numbers, 2.34 and 9.60, the middle point is (2.34+9.60)/2 = 5.97. Official: pI = (pKa1 + pKa2)/2. Why wrong: Picking 6.34 would be like averaging 2.34 and 10.34, which is incorrect.

## bp832-4 Which of the following statements about the pKa values is incorrect?
Correct: C
Analogy 10‑yr‑old: Think of pKa like a score that tells you how strong a bond is; it doesn’t have a unit like moles. Official: pKa is a dimensionless quantity derived from the acid dissociation constant (Ka). Why wrong: Saying it’s measured in moles per liter confuses it with concentration.

## bp832-5 Which statement best describes the relationship between a protein’s isoelectric point (pI) and its migration during isoelectric focusing?
Correct: C
Analogy 10‑yr‑old: Imagine a ball that stops rolling when the slope is flat. The pH gradient is the slope; the protein stops when the slope (net charge) is zero. Official: At pI, net charge = 0, so the protein has no electrophoretic mobility. Why wrong: Thinking it moves toward anode/cathode ignores the zero‑charge condition.
