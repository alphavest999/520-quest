# BP1209

Researchers studied the effect of caffeine on working memory performance in college students. Forty‑eight participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: a placebo group (0 mg caffeine), a low‑dose group (50 mg caffeine), and a high‑dose group (200 mg caffeine). After ingestion, participants completed a 30‑minute n‑back task while their reaction times (RTs) and accuracy were recorded. The mean RTs (in milliseconds) and standard deviations (SD) for each group are presented in Table 1. The researchers hypothesized that moderate caffeine would improve working memory, reflected by faster RTs and higher accuracy, while excessive caffeine might produce a U‑shaped performance curve. Additionally, the study collected subjective alertness ratings on a 7‑point Likert scale. The results showed a statistically significant difference in RTs among the groups (p < 0.01). The low‑dose group had the fastest mean RT, whereas the high‑dose group had the slowest. Accuracy rates followed a similar pattern, with the low‑dose group achieving the highest percentage correct. The authors concluded that a moderate caffeine dose optimizes working‑memory performance, supporting the inverted‑U hypothesis of arousal and cognition.

A crossover design lets each participant serve as their own control, reducing variability from individual caffeine tolerance.
