# BP1336

Researchers investigated the effect of caffeine on cognitive performance in a controlled laboratory setting. A total of 120 healthy adults (age 20–35) were randomly assigned to one of four groups: placebo, low-dose caffeine (100 mg), moderate-dose caffeine (200 mg), and high-dose caffeine (300 mg). Each participant completed a 30‑minute psychomotor vigilance task (PVT) after ingestion. Reaction times were recorded in milliseconds, and the number of lapses (reaction time > 500 ms) was counted. The study aimed to determine whether increasing caffeine dosage correlates with improved vigilance and whether there is a threshold beyond which performance declines. Participants were instructed to abstain from caffeine for 12 hours prior to the experiment. The data were analyzed using a one‑way ANOVA followed by post‑hoc Tukey tests. The researchers hypothesized a dose‑dependent improvement up to 200 mg, with possible plateau or decline at 300 mg due to overstimulation.

## bp1336_q2 If the researchers wanted to test whether the observed improvement in reaction time at 200 mg is statistically significant compared to the placebo group, which experimental design element is most critical?
Correct: A
Blinding participants prevents expectancy effects that could bias performance, which is essential for attributing changes in reaction time to caffeine dosage rather than placebo or nocebo effects.

## bp1336_q3 Assuming the standard deviations reported are accurate, calculate the approximate 95% confidence interval for the mean reaction time of the moderate‑dose group.
Correct: C
Standard error = SD/√n = 35/√120 ≈ 3.19 ms. 95% CI ≈ 310 ± 1.96×3.19 ≈ 310 ± 6.25 → 303.75–316.25 ms, which rounds to 305–325 ms.

## bp1336_q4 Which of the following is a potential trap when interpreting the study’s findings?
Correct: B
The study’s sample is limited to healthy adults aged 20–35; individual metabolic differences can influence caffeine effects, so generalizing causation without accounting for these differences is a trap.
