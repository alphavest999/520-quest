# BP1720

Researchers studied the effect of caffeine on cognitive performance in a double‑blind, randomized controlled trial. A total of 120 healthy adults aged 20‑35 were recruited and randomly assigned to one of three groups: placebo (n=40), low‑dose caffeine (100 mg, n=40), or high‑dose caffeine (200 mg, n=40). After a 30‑minute absorption period, participants completed a series of computerized tasks designed to assess reaction time, working memory, and sustained attention. The primary outcome was mean reaction time (ms) on a simple visual‑stimulus task. Secondary outcomes included accuracy on a digit‑span task and the number of lapses on a continuous performance test. Researchers hypothesized that caffeine would produce a dose‑dependent improvement in reaction time, with the high‑dose group showing the greatest reduction relative to placebo. The study also aimed to explore whether individual differences in baseline caffeine consumption moderated the treatment effect.

## Q1 Which group had the fastest mean reaction time?
Correct: C
The high‑dose caffeine group had a mean reaction time of 310 ms, the lowest among the three groups, indicating the fastest responses.

## Q2 Which design feature best controls for expectancy effects?
Correct: B
A double‑blind procedure prevents both participants and researchers from knowing group assignments, thereby controlling for expectancy effects.

## Q3 What is the percent reduction in mean reaction time for the high‑dose group compared to placebo?
Correct: C
Reduction = (350 ms – 310 ms) / 350 ms = 0.1143, or approximately 11%.

## Q4 Which of the following is a potential confound that was NOT addressed by the study design?
Correct: A
The design did not control for participants’ habitual caffeine intake, which could influence the response to the experimental doses.

## Q5 Which statistical test would be most appropriate to compare reaction times across the three groups?
Correct: B
One‑way ANOVA tests for mean differences among three independent groups on a continuous outcome.
