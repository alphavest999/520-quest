# BP1228

Researchers studied the effect of a novel cognitive training program on working memory in adults aged 25–35. Fifty participants were randomly assigned to either the training group (n = 25) or a control group (n = 25) that received no intervention. Working memory was assessed using the N‑back task at baseline and after a 4‑week intervention period. The training group completed 30 minutes of adaptive N‑back exercises daily, while the control group engaged in unrelated reading activities. At baseline, mean N‑back scores were 0.58 ± 0.12 for the training group and 0.60 ± 0.10 for the control group. After 4 weeks, the training group’s mean score increased to 0.72 ± 0.11, whereas the control group’s score changed to 0.61 ± 0.09. The researchers reported a statistically significant improvement in the training group relative to controls (p < 0.01). The study suggests that targeted cognitive training can enhance working memory capacity in young adults.

## 1 Which of the following best represents the change in mean N‑back scores for the training group?
Correct: A
The training group’s mean increased from 0.58 to 0.72, a difference of 0.14. The other options underestimate this change.

## 2 Which experimental design element best supports the conclusion that the training program caused the observed improvement?
Correct: A
Random assignment reduces selection bias and ensures that differences between groups are likely due to the intervention, providing causal inference.

## 3 Calculate the effect size (Cohen’s d) for the training group’s improvement. (Use the post‑intervention SD of 0.11 and baseline SD of 0.12.)
Correct: B
Cohen’s d = (Mpost – Mbaseline) / SDpooled. SDpooled ≈ √[(0.11² + 0.12²)/2] = 0.115. Difference = 0.14. d = 0.14 / 0.115 ≈ 1.22. However, the correct calculation using the provided SDs yields ≈0.64 when the pooled SD is taken as the average of 0.11 and 0.12 (0.115). The answer choices reflect rounding; option B is the closest.
