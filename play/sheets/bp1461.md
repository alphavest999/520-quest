# BP1461

Researchers at the University of Greenfield investigated the effects of a new cognitive training program on working memory performance in adults aged 25–40. Sixty participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: (1) the training group received the program for 12 weeks, (2) an active control group engaged in general educational activities for the same duration, and (3) a passive control group received no intervention. Working memory was assessed using the n-back task (2-back and 3-back conditions) at baseline, mid-intervention (6 weeks), and post-intervention (12 weeks). The training group showed a mean improvement of 12.4% in 3-back accuracy from baseline to post-intervention, compared to 4.7% in the active control and 1.2% in the passive control. Standard deviations ranged from 3.1 to 5.8 across groups. The researchers concluded that the training program significantly enhanced working memory capacity, suggesting potential applications for cognitive rehabilitation.

## bp1461_q2 If the researchers wanted to test whether the training program had a greater effect on 3-back accuracy than the active control, which experimental design element is most critical?
Correct: C
Random assignment minimizes confounding variables and ensures that differences observed are likely due to the intervention. While equal baseline accuracy is important, randomization is the key design element for causal inference.

## bp1461_q4 Which of the following statements is a potential trap when interpreting the reported 12.4% improvement for the training group?
Correct: B
A 12.4% improvement may partly reflect regression to the mean, especially if participants had unusually low baseline scores. This is a common trap in interpreting pre‑post changes without controlling for baseline variability.
