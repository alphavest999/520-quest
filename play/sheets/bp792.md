# BP792

Researchers at the University of Metropolis performed a titration experiment to determine the isoelectric points (pI) of four amino acids—lysine, aspartic acid, serine, and glutamine—using a 0.01 M buffer solution. Each amino acid (0.5 mmol) was dissolved in 10 mL of distilled water, and the pH was recorded after incremental additions of 0.1 M NaOH. The titration curves displayed distinct buffering regions corresponding to the amino and carboxyl groups, as well as side‑chain functional groups. The researchers plotted pH versus volume of NaOH added and identified the pH at which the slope of the curve was minimal, indicating the pI. The experiment also included a control where the ionic strength was increased to 0.1 M NaCl to assess its effect on the buffering capacity. Data analysis involved calculating the average of the pKa values of the ionizable groups for each amino acid, then averaging the two relevant pKa values to obtain the pI. The results were compared to literature values to validate the experimental approach.

| Amino Acid | Side‑Chain pKa | pI |
|------------|----------------|----|
| Lysine     | 10.5           | 9.74 |
| Aspartic Acid | 3.9         | 3.65 |
| Serine     | 13.7           | 5.7 |
| Glutamine  | 9.6            | 9.06 |


## bp792-1 From the titration data provided, which pH value most accurately represents the isoelectric point of lysine?
Correct: C
Analogy 10‑yr: Think of lysine as a seesaw that balances at pH 9.7—just like a child balancing on a see‑saw at the middle point. Official: The pI is the pH where net charge is zero, calculated as the average of the two pKa values for the amino and side‑chain groups (10.5 and 9.4). Why wrong: Option B (pH 9.0) is too low; the curve shows a plateau near 9.7, not 9.0.

## bp792-2 If the researchers increased the ionic strength of the solution from 0.01 M to 0.1 M NaCl, what would be the most likely effect on the observed pI of aspartic acid?
Correct: C
Analogy 10‑yr: Imagine a balance scale that stays level even if you add salt to the water; the scale’s balance point doesn’t change. Official: Ionic strength affects the activity coefficients of ions but not the intrinsic pKa values, so the pI remains essentially the same. Why wrong: Option A or B incorrectly assumes a shift in pKa values due to ionic strength.

## bp792-3 Calculate the pI of serine using the provided side‑chain pKa value.
Correct: A
Analogy 10‑yr: Think of pI as the middle point between the carboxyl pKa (2.2) and the amino pKa (9.2); the middle is 5.7. Official: pI = (pKa1 + pKa2)/2 = (2.2 + 9.2)/2 = 5.7. Why wrong: Option B mistakenly averages with the side‑chain pKa, which is not used for serine.
