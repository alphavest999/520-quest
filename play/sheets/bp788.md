# BP788

Researchers at the University of Metropolis investigated the titration curves of four amino acids—glycine, alanine, lysine, and glutamic acid—using a calibrated pH meter and a 0.1 M HCl/NaOH titrant. Each amino acid solution (10 mM) was titrated from pH 1.0 to pH 12.5 while recording the net charge of the molecule at each pH point. The experiment aimed to determine the isoelectric point (pI) of each amino acid and to compare the experimental curves with theoretical predictions based on known pKa values. The data were plotted as net charge versus pH, revealing sigmoidal curves with distinct inflection points corresponding to the dissociation of the carboxyl, amino, and side‑chain groups. The researchers also performed a second set of titrations at 0.5 M NaCl to assess the effect of ionic strength on the curves. The resulting tables and graphs were used to validate the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation and to illustrate how electrostatic shielding influences protonation equilibria.

## bp788-01 Which variable is the independent variable in the titration experiment described?
Correct: A
Analogy: A 10‑year‑old thinks the experiment’s goal is to find the net charge. Official: The independent variable is the one the researcher manipulates—here, the pH of the solution. Why wrong: The net charge is the dependent variable that changes in response to the pH.

## bp788-02 If the researchers increased the ionic strength of the solution to 1.0 M NaCl, what would most likely happen to the titration curve?
Correct: B
Analogy: A 10‑year‑old might think adding salt makes the solution more acidic. Official: Higher ionic strength shields charges, reducing the effective pKa of ionizable groups and shifting the titration curve to the right. Why wrong: The salt does not increase acidity; it stabilizes the charged species, raising the pH at which dissociation occurs.

## bp788-03 Calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of lysine (pKa1 = 2.18, pKa2 = 9.74, pKa3 = 10.53).
Correct: B
Analogy: A 10‑year‑old might average all three pKa values. Official: For a basic amino acid, pI is the average of the two pKa values that bracket the net charge of zero. Here, pI = (9.74 + 10.53)/2 = 10.135 ≈ 10.13. Why wrong: Averaging all three pKa values gives an incorrect value.

## bp788-04 Which of the following is NOT a correct unit for expressing a pKa value?
Correct: D
Analogy: A 10‑year‑old might think any concentration unit works. Official: pKa is a dimensionless ratio of activities; it is expressed in mol L⁻¹ (M) or mol m⁻³, but not in mol kg⁻¹. Why wrong: Using mass units misrepresents the activity ratio.
