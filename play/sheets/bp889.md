# BP889

Researchers investigated the acid–base properties of four amino acids—glycine, alanine, lysine, and glutamic acid—using potentiometric titration. In each experiment, the amino acid was dissolved in 0.1 M NaCl, and the pH was recorded as incremental amounts of 0.1 M HCl or NaOH were added. The titration curves revealed three distinct pKa values for each amino acid: the carboxyl group (pKa1), the α‑amino group (pKaR), and, where applicable, a side‑chain functional group (pKa side). The data were plotted as pH versus volume of titrant to determine the precise pKa values. This approach allows for direct comparison of the intrinsic acidity of the side chains and the determination of each amino acid’s isoelectric point (pI). The experiment also highlights the importance of buffer selection and ionic strength on the accuracy of pKa measurements, which are critical for understanding protein folding, enzyme catalysis, and drug design.

## 889-1 Which amino acid has the highest side‑chain pKa among those studied?
Correct: C
Analogy: Imagine each side chain is a door that opens (deprotonates) when the room’s temperature (pH) rises. Lysine’s door opens only at a very high temperature, so its side‑chain pKa is highest. Official: The correct answer is Lysine. Why wrong: Glycine and alanine lack side‑chain pKa values, and glutamic acid’s side chain has a lower pKa (4.25).

## 889-2 If the goal is to determine the pKa of lysine’s side‑chain amine, which pH range should the buffer system cover?
Correct: C
Analogy: Think of a scale that measures how easily a door opens as the room gets hotter. To catch the moment lysine’s side‑chain door opens (pKa ≈ 10.5), you need to monitor temperatures around 8–12. Official: The buffer should span 8–12. Why wrong: Lower ranges miss the deprotonation event; higher ranges are unnecessary and waste reagents.

## 889-3 Using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, what fraction of lysine’s side‑chain amine is deprotonated at pH 9.0? (pKa = 10.53)
Correct: B
Analogy: Picture a seesaw where one side is the protonated form and the other the deprotonated form. At pH 9, the seesaw is heavily tilted toward the protonated side, leaving only about 3% on the deprotonated side. Official: The correct fraction is ~2.9%. Why wrong: 0.29% underestimates the deprotonated form; 10.5% and 50% overestimate it.

## 889-4 Which of the following units is incorrectly used in reporting the titration data?
Correct: C
Analogy: Think of measuring temperature in a recipe—you’d use Celsius or Fahrenheit, not Kelvin. In titration, temperature is recorded in °C, not K. Official: Kelvin is the wrong unit for pH or temperature in this context. Why wrong: The other units (moles, molarity, grams) are standard for titration data.

## 889-5 Which concept is most directly linked to the determination of the isoelectric point (pI) in this study?
Correct: B
Analogy: The pI is like the balance point on a seesaw of charges; the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation tells you how the charges shift with pH. Official: The correct link is the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. Why wrong: Michaelis–Menten, inhibition, and folding thermodynamics are unrelated to pI calculation.
