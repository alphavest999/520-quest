# BP1322

Researchers at the University of Midvale investigated the impact of caffeine on reaction time among 120 adolescents aged 13–17. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: a placebo, a low dose (50 mg), a medium dose (100 mg), or a high dose (200 mg) of caffeine. After a 30‑minute absorption period, each subject completed a computerized Go/No‑Go task, and mean reaction times (RT) were recorded. The study aimed to determine whether increasing caffeine dosage linearly reduced RT and to assess potential dose‑related side effects. The researchers also measured heart rate and reported subjective alertness to explore physiological correlates. Data were analyzed using ANOVA and post‑hoc Tukey tests, with significance set at p < .05. The findings suggested that higher caffeine doses were associated with faster RTs, but the high dose group also reported increased jitteriness. The study contributes to understanding how stimulants affect adolescent cognition and informs guidelines for caffeine consumption in youth. Future research should examine long‑term effects and interactions with sleep patterns.

## bp1322_q2 Which experimental design best controls for individual differences in baseline reaction time?
Correct: B
A within‑subjects design would have each participant experience all caffeine conditions, thereby controlling for individual baseline differences. The study used random assignment to separate groups, which is a between‑subjects design; however, the question asks for the best control, which is within‑subjects.
