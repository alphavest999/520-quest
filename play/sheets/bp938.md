# BP938

Researchers investigated the acid–base properties of four amino acids—glycine, alanine, lysine, and glutamic acid—by titrating each with standardized NaOH and recording the pH at each equivalence point. The titration curves revealed distinct pKa values corresponding to the carboxyl and amino functional groups. The table below summarizes the experimentally determined pKa values and the calculated isoelectric points (pI). The study aimed to correlate these physicochemical parameters with the solubility behavior of the amino acids in aqueous solutions at physiological pH. The authors also examined how the presence of a strong base or acid shifts the pH at which each amino acid becomes neutral, thereby affecting its precipitation tendency. This data set is commonly used in biochemistry courses to illustrate the concept of pI and to practice calculations involving Henderson–Hasselbalch and ionization fractions.

| Amino Acid | pKa1 (COOH) | pKa2 (NH3⁺) | pI |
|------------|-------------|-------------|-----|
| Glycine    | 2.34        | 9.60        | 5.97 |
| Alanine    | 2.35        | 9.69        | 6.02 |
| Lysine     | 2.18        | 9.06        | 9.62 |
| Glutamic Acid | 2.19     | 4.25        | 3.22 |

## bp938-1 Based on the table, which amino acid has the highest isoelectric point (pI)?
Correct: C
Analogy 10‑yr: Imagine each amino acid as a see‑saw that balances at a certain pH. The higher the pI, the higher the see‑saw’s balance point. Official: The pI is the pH at which the net charge of the amino acid is zero. Why wrong: Thinking the pI is just the average of the two pKa values works only for simple amino acids; for those with additional ionizable groups (like lysine’s side chain), the calculation differs.

## bp938-2 If a strong base is added to a solution of lysine at pH 7.0, which species will precipitate first as the solution’s pH rises toward the lysine’s pI?
Correct: B
Analogy 10‑yr: Picture lysine as a balloon that expands as you add base. It first becomes neutral (zwitterion) before it starts to shrink (precipitate). Official: The species with the net zero charge (zwitterion) has the lowest solubility near the pI and will precipitate first. Why wrong: Assuming the fully protonated or deprotonated forms precipitate first ignores the fact that precipitation occurs when the net charge is zero.

## bp938-3 Using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, calculate the fraction of lysine that is protonated at its amino group (NH3⁺) when the solution pH is 7.0.
Correct: C
Analogy 10‑yr: Think of the amino group as a light switch that turns on (protonated) when the pH is below its pKa. Official: Fraction protonated = 1 / (1 + 10^(pH–pKa)). Plugging pH 7.0 and pKa 9.06 gives 1 / (1 + 10^(7–9.06)) ≈ 0.88. Why wrong: Using the wrong pKa (e.g., the carboxyl pKa) would give a much lower fraction.

## bp938-4 Which of the following statements about the units of pKa is correct?
Correct: B
Analogy 10‑yr: Think of pKa like a recipe number that tells you how much of something you need, but it doesn’t have a unit like grams or liters. Official: pKa = –log10(Ka); Ka is a concentration ratio (M/M), so the log makes it dimensionless. Why wrong: Confusing pKa with the equilibrium constant (Ka) or with thermodynamic energy units.
