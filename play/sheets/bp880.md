# BP880

Researchers investigated the acid–base properties of five common amino acids—glycine, alanine, glutamic acid, lysine, and histidine—using potentiometric titration. Each amino acid was dissolved in 0.01 M aqueous solution and titrated with 0.1 M NaOH while the pH was recorded at 25 °C. A glass electrode calibrated with standard buffers (pH 4.0, 7.0, 10.0) was employed to ensure accurate measurements. The resulting titration curves displayed the characteristic inflection points corresponding to the dissociation of the α‑carboxyl, side‑chain, and α‑ammonium groups. From the curves, the researchers calculated the pKa values for each functional group and derived the isoelectric point (pI) as the average of the two pKa values that bracket a net zero charge. The data are summarized in the table below. This experimental setup allows comparison of how side‑chain chemistry influences the overall charge state of the amino acid across the physiological pH range.

| Amino Acid | α‑COOH pKa | Side‑chain pKa | α‑NH3⁺ pKa | pI |
|------------|------------|----------------|------------|-----|
| Glycine    | 2.34       | –              | 9.60       | 5.97 |
| Alanine    | 2.35       | –              | 9.87       | 6.11 |
| Glutamic Acid | 2.19   | 4.25           | 9.60       | 4.25 |
| Lysine     | 2.18       | 10.53          | 10.53      | 9.74 |
| Histidine  | 1.82       | 6.00           | 9.17       | 7.59 |

## bp880-01 Which amino acid has the highest isoelectric point (pI) based on the data presented?
Correct: D
Analogy for a 10‑year‑old: Imagine each amino acid as a tiny house with a door (the side chain). The pI is the exact temperature at which the door is neither open nor closed—so the house has no net charge. For Lysine, the side chain stays positively charged until a very high pH, making its pI the highest. Official: The pI is calculated as the average of the two pKa values that surround a net zero charge; Lysine’s side‑chain pKa (10.53) and α‑NH3⁺ pKa (10.53) give the highest pI. Why the others are wrong: Glycine and Alanine lack a side‑chain pKa, so their pIs are lower; Glutamic Acid’s side chain is acidic and lowers its pI.

## bp880-02 If the titration experiment were performed using 0.1 M HCl as the titrant instead of NaOH, how would the shape of the titration curves change?
Correct: A
Analogy for a 10‑year‑old: Think of the titration curve as a slide. If you pour acid instead of base, the slide starts lower and stays lower. Official: Adding HCl neutralizes the amino acid’s basic groups, decreasing pH at every point, so the entire curve moves left (lower pH). Why the others are wrong: The titrant concentration is the same, but the direction of the titration (acid vs base) determines the pH trend; the slope change in D is unrelated to the titrant type.

## bp880-03 Calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of glutamic acid using the pKa values provided.
Correct: A
Analogy for a 10‑year‑old: The pI is like the middle of two numbers that make the charge zero. For glutamic acid, the two relevant pKa values are 2.19 (α‑COOH) and 4.25 (side chain). The average is (2.19+4.25)/2 = 3.22, but because the side chain is acidic, the pI is the lower of the two, 4.25. Official: For acidic side chains, the pI equals the side‑chain pKa. Why the others are wrong: 5.00 and 6.00 are averages of unrelated pKa values; 7.59 is the pI of histidine.

## bp880-04 Which of the following statements about the effect of temperature on pKa is FALSE?
Correct: C
Analogy for a 10‑year‑old: Imagine a thermostat that changes how easily a switch turns on or off; temperature can shift that switch point. Official: Temperature affects the Gibbs free energy of dissociation, altering pKa for both acids and bases. Statement C is false because temperature does influence side‑chain pKa values. A and B are true trends, and D reflects the variable magnitude.

## bp880-05 Which statement best describes the relationship between a side‑chain pKa and its charge at physiological pH (7.4)?
Correct: C
Analogy for a 10‑year‑old: Think of the pKa as a threshold; if the pH is above the threshold, the group loses a proton and becomes negative. Official: For acidic side chains (pKa < 7.4), deprotonation occurs, giving a negative charge. For basic side chains (pKa > 7.4), protonation occurs, giving a positive charge. Statement C correctly captures the acidic case; A and D mix up charge signs, and B incorrectly states neutrality.
