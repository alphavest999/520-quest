# BP1077

Researchers studied the effect of a novel dietary supplement on the growth rate of laboratory mice over a 12-week period. Twenty-four mice were randomly assigned to one of three groups: control (no supplement), low-dose (5 mg/kg/day), and high-dose (15 mg/kg/day). Body weight was recorded weekly. Table 1 summarizes the mean body weight (in grams) and standard deviation for each group at weeks 0, 4, 8, and 12. The study aimed to determine whether the supplement accelerates growth and if a dose-response relationship exists. Additionally, the researchers measured serum insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF‑1) levels at week 12 to explore a potential mechanism. The data suggest a trend toward increased weight gain in the high-dose group, but variability within groups complicates interpretation. The authors conclude that while the supplement shows promise, further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm efficacy.

## bp1077_q1 Which group showed the greatest mean weight gain from week 0 to week 12?
Correct: C
The high‑dose group increased from 20.7 g to 44.8 g, a gain of 24.1 g, the largest among the three groups.

## bp1077_q2 Which study design would best assess causality between supplement dosage and weight gain?
Correct: B
Randomization controls for confounders and establishes a causal relationship between dosage and outcome.

## bp1077_q3 What was the average weekly weight gain for the high‑dose group?
Correct: B
Average weekly gain = (44.8 g – 20.7 g) / 12 ≈ 2.0 g/week.

## bp1077_q4 Which of the following statements is a common misinterpretation of the data?
Correct: A
The study is observational; correlation does not prove causation. The supplement may be associated with weight gain but cannot be confirmed as the cause.

## bp1077_q5 Which broader biological concept is most relevant to interpreting the IGF‑1 findings?
Correct: A
IGF‑1 is a key hormone that promotes growth; higher levels in the high‑dose group suggest a hormonal mechanism.
