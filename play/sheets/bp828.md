# BP828

Researchers at the University of Metropolis performed a systematic titration of four amino acids—glycine, alanine, lysine, and glutamic acid—to determine their pKa values and isoelectric points (pI). Each amino acid solution (0.1 M) was titrated with 0.1 M NaOH while monitoring pH with a calibrated glass electrode. The titrant volume was recorded at each inflection point. The experiment was repeated at three different ionic strengths (0.01, 0.1, and 1.0 M NaCl) to assess the effect of ionic strength on apparent pKa values. Data were plotted as pH versus volume of NaOH added, and the inflection points were identified using the first derivative method. The resulting pKa values were compared to literature values to evaluate experimental accuracy. The study also calculated pI values for each amino acid by averaging the pKa values of the ionizable groups that bracket the net zero charge. The authors concluded that increased ionic strength slightly lowered the apparent pKa values, consistent with Debye–Hückel theory.

## 828-01 Which variable was most likely the independent variable in the titration experiment described?
Correct: B
A 10‑year‑old would say the independent variable is the pH because it changes. The official answer is the volume of NaOH added—this is what the experimenters varied to observe the pH response. The pH is the dependent variable that results from changing the titrant volume.

## 828-02 If the researchers increased the ionic strength of the solution, what would most likely happen to the measured pKa values?
Correct: B
A 10‑year‑old might think higher salt means stronger acids, but the official answer is that higher ionic strength lowers apparent pKa values because ion–ion interactions reduce activity coefficients. This is a subtle effect predicted by Debye–Hückel theory.

## 828-03 Calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of lysine using the pKa values provided.
Correct: C
A 10‑year‑old might add the three pKa values together, but the official method is to average the two pKa values that surround the net zero charge. For lysine, (pKa2 + pKa3)/2 = (9.74 + 10.53)/2 ≈ 10.14. Adding all three would give a wrong answer.

## 828-04 Which of the following is a common mistake when interpreting the pI values of amino acids?
Correct: C
A 10‑year‑old might think the side‑chain pKa always determines the pI, but the official rule is that pI is the average of the two pKa values that bracket the net zero charge. For lysine, the side‑chain pKa is part of that average, but it is not the sole determinant. Misidentifying the side‑chain as the pI leads to incorrect values.
