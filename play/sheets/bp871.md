# BP871

Researchers performed a titration experiment to determine the pKa and pI values of glycine, lysine, and aspartic acid. Each amino acid (0.1 M) was dissolved in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). A calibrated NaOH solution (0.1 M) was added in 0.5 mL increments while the pH was recorded after each addition. The resulting titration curves were plotted as pH versus volume of NaOH added. The midpoint of each buffering region (where the pH change per added volume is minimal) was taken as the pKa of the corresponding functional group. The pI was calculated by averaging the pH values at which the net charge of the amino acid is zero. The experiment was repeated three times to assess reproducibility. The data were tabulated and used to validate Henderson–Hasselbalch calculations and to illustrate the influence of ionic strength on acid–base equilibria.

## bp871-1 Which variable was manipulated by the researchers during the titration?
Correct: B

Why wrong: The 10‑yr analogy is too vague and does not specify that the experimenter controls the NaOH volume, which is the true independent variable here.

## bp871-2 If the ionic strength of the buffer were increased, what would happen to the measured pKa values?
Correct: B
Analogy 10‑yr: "More salt in the water makes the acids act a little stronger." Official: "Higher ionic strength shields charges, shifting pKa values to higher numbers (less dissociation)." Why wrong: The 10‑yr explanation oversimplifies by suggesting acids become stronger, whereas the correct effect is a modest increase in pKa.

## bp871-3 Using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, what is the pH when the α‑COOH group of glycine is 50 % neutralized?
Correct: A
Analogy 10‑yr: "Half of the acid is turned into its base, so the pH equals the pKa." Official: "At 50 % neutralization, [A⁻]/[HA] = 1, so pH = pKa + log(1) = pKa." Why wrong: The 10‑yr explanation is correct in principle but omits the logarithmic relationship and the fact that the pKa value itself is the pH at 50 % neutralization.

## bp871-4 Which statement correctly describes the units of a pKa value?
Correct: D
Analogy 10‑yr: "pKa is like a number that tells you how strong an acid is." Official: "pKa is the negative logarithm of the acid dissociation constant (Ka); it is a pure number with no units." Why wrong: The 10‑yr explanation ignores that pKa is unitless, leading students to incorrectly associate it with energy or concentration units.
