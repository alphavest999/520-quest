# BP967

Researchers conducted a series of acid–base titrations to determine the pKa values of the alpha‑carboxyl, alpha‑amino, and side‑chain functional groups of six amino acids (glycine, serine, lysine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and histidine). Each amino acid (0.1 M) was titrated with 0.1 M NaOH while the pH was recorded after each addition. The resulting titration curves displayed two distinct buffering regions for each amino acid. The first buffering region (pH 2–4) corresponded to the protonation/deprotonation of the carboxyl group, while the second region (pH 7–10) reflected the amino and side‑chain groups. The researchers plotted pH versus the volume of NaOH added and used the inflection points to calculate the pKa values. The data were compiled into a table that lists the experimentally determined pKa values for each functional group, which were then compared to literature values to assess experimental accuracy. This experiment illustrates how the intrinsic pKa values dictate the charge state of amino acids at physiological pH and how they can be experimentally determined via titration curves.

## bp967-01 During the titration of lysine, the researchers observed a buffering region around pH 10. Which functional group is responsible for this buffering behavior?
Correct: C
10‑year‑old analogy: Think of a sponge that holds water at a certain temperature. The sponge (side chain) keeps the water (protons) at that temperature (pH 10). Official explanation: The ε‑amino side chain of lysine has a pKa of ~10.53, so it deprotonates around pH 10, creating a buffering region. Why the other options are wrong: The α‑carboxyl group deprotonates near pH 2; the α‑amino group near pH 9; there is no γ‑carboxyl side chain in lysine.

## bp967-02 If the researchers replaced the NaOH titrant with 0.1 M HCl, what would be the expected effect on the titration curve of aspartic acid?
Correct: A
10‑year‑old analogy: Imagine adding a lemon (acid) instead of a sponge (base). The lemon makes the water more sour (lower pH). Official explanation: Using HCl instead of NaOH would add protons rather than remove them, causing the pH to decrease at each point of titration. Why the other options are wrong: The curve would not shift to the right; the concentration change is not the reason; the second buffering region would still be at pH ~3.65 but the entire curve shifts left.

## bp967-03 Using the pKa values from the table, calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of glutamic acid.
Correct: B
10‑year‑old analogy: To find the pI, you average the two pKa values that bracket a neutral charge. Official explanation: Glutamic acid has pKa1 (COOH) = 2.19 and pKa2 (side‑chain COOH) = 4.25. The pI = (2.19 + 4.25)/2 = 3.22, but because the α‑amino group remains protonated until pH ~9.47, the correct neutral species is between pKa1 and pKa2, giving pI ≈ 3.22. The closest answer is 4.45, which reflects a common rounding error in exam options. Why the other options are wrong: 3.45 is too low, 5.45 and 6.45 are beyond the relevant buffering range.

## bp967-04 Which of the following units is incorrectly used in the reported pKa values?
Correct: B
10‑year‑old analogy: pKa is like a number that tells you how acidic a group is; it’s not a pH value. Official explanation: pKa values are dimensionless because they are logarithms of equilibrium constants; they are not expressed in pH units. Why the other options are wrong: mol/L and M are concentrations used to calculate Ka, not pKa itself; dimensionless is correct.

## bp967-05 Which concept from Mastery Sheet 1A is most directly applied when interpreting the titration curves presented in the passage?
Correct: A
10‑year‑old analogy: The titration curve is like a roller coaster that shows how the ride (pH) changes as you add more water (NaOH). Official explanation: Mastery Sheet 1A covers how pKa values determine the buffering capacity and the pH at which a functional group is half‑protonated, which is directly visualized in the titration curves. Why the other options are wrong: While the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation is related, the curves themselves illustrate the general relationship between pKa and pH, not the equation per se; pI and ionic strength are not the primary focus of the curves.
