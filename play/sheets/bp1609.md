# BP1609

Researchers studied the effect of three novel attentional training protocols on reaction time in a laboratory setting. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four groups: a control group that received no training, and three treatment groups (A, B, and C) that each received a different training protocol. After a 4‑week intervention period, participants completed a computerized visual‑search task while their reaction times were recorded. The mean reaction times (in milliseconds) and standard deviations for each group are presented in Table 1. The data suggest that all training protocols reduced reaction time relative to the control, with Treatment B yielding the greatest improvement. The researchers plan to analyze whether the differences among treatments are statistically significant and to explore the underlying neural mechanisms. This study exemplifies how controlled experimental designs can isolate the effects of specific interventions on cognitive performance.

## 1 Which group had the fastest average reaction time?
Correct: C
The mean reaction time for Treatment B is 1050 ms, which is lower (i.e., faster) than the other groups (Control 1200 ms, Treatment A 1100 ms, Treatment C 1150 ms). Lower reaction time indicates faster performance.

## 2 Which experimental design best tests the effect of the treatments on reaction time?
Correct: A
A randomized controlled trial (RCT) randomly assigns participants to treatment or control groups, controlling for confounding variables and allowing causal inference about the effect of the treatments on reaction time.

## 3 Calculate the percent reduction in mean reaction time from Control to Treatment B.
Correct: B
Percent reduction = (1200 – 1050) / 1200 × 100 = 150 / 1200 × 100 = 12.5%. Thus option B is correct.

## 4 Which statement is a common misinterpretation of the table?
Correct: B
The table only reports descriptive statistics; it does not provide p‑values or confidence intervals. Assuming that the observed mean differences are statistically significant without inferential analysis is a trap.

## 5 How does the observed data support the theory of attentional allocation?
Correct: B
In attentional allocation theory, a lower attentional load allows participants to process stimuli more efficiently, resulting in faster reaction times. The data show that the training protocols, which presumably reduce attentional load, lead to faster responses.
