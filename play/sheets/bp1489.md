# BP1489

Researchers studied the effect of caffeine on reaction time in a controlled laboratory setting. A total of 120 healthy adults aged 18–35 were randomly assigned to one of four groups: (A) placebo, (B) 50 mg caffeine, (C) 100 mg caffeine, and (D) 150 mg caffeine. Each participant completed a computerized simple reaction time task, consisting of 200 trials, after ingestion of the assigned beverage. Mean reaction times (RT) and standard deviations (SD) were recorded for each group. The study aimed to determine whether increasing caffeine dosage leads to a linear decrease in RT and to assess inter-individual variability. The researchers also collected baseline heart rate and sleep quality data to control for potential confounders. Statistical analysis included one-way ANOVA and post-hoc Tukey tests. The results suggested a dose-dependent improvement in RT, with the 150 mg group showing the greatest reduction compared to placebo. However, the variability within the 150 mg group was higher, indicating possible individual differences in caffeine metabolism. These findings contribute to the understanding of stimulant effects on cognitive performance and have implications for occupational safety and athletic performance.
