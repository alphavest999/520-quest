# BP1750

Researchers investigated the effect of a novel cognitive training program on working memory performance in adults aged 25–35. Sixty participants were randomly assigned to either the training group (n = 30) or a control group (n = 30) that received no intervention. Over a four‑week period, the training group completed 45‑minute sessions twice weekly, while the control group maintained their usual activities. Working memory was assessed before and after the intervention using the n‑back task, recording reaction time (RT) and accuracy. Table 1 summarizes the mean RT (in milliseconds) and standard deviation (SD) for each group at baseline and post‑intervention. The researchers hypothesized that the training group would exhibit a statistically significant reduction in RT compared to controls. The study aimed to contribute to the literature on cognitive enhancement and to inform future interventions for improving executive function.

## bp1750-q1 Which of the following statements best describes the data presented in Table 1?
Correct: C
The training group’s mean RT decreased from 650 to 590 ms (a 60‑ms improvement). The control group’s mean RT decreased from 648 to 640 ms (an 8‑ms improvement). The SD values show a slight decrease for the training group and a negligible change for the control group, so option D is incorrect.

## bp1750-q2 Which of the following experimental designs best matches the study described?
Correct: C
Participants were randomly assigned to either the training or control group and remained in that group for the entire study period, which is characteristic of a randomized controlled trial with parallel groups. The study was not blinded, did not involve a placebo, nor did participants switch groups.

## bp1750-q3 Calculate the absolute change in mean RT for the training group from baseline to post‑intervention.
Correct: B
Absolute change = baseline mean – post‑intervention mean = 650 – 590 = 60 ms.

## bp1750-q4 Which of the following statements is a common trap when interpreting the study’s results?
Correct: A
The study lacks a placebo or active control, so attributing improvement solely to the training without accounting for placebo or expectancy effects is a common interpretive trap.
