# BP798

Researchers at the University of Chemville studied the titration of glycine, an amino acid with both carboxyl and amino functional groups. In a controlled experiment, 10 mL of 0.1 M glycine solution was placed in a 25 mL beaker and titrated with 0.1 M NaOH while a calibrated pH meter recorded the pH after each addition. The experiment was repeated three times to ensure reproducibility. The resulting data were plotted as a titration curve, revealing the two buffering regions corresponding to the dissociation of the carboxyl group (pKa ≈ 2.34) and the protonated amino group (pKa ≈ 9.60). The midpoint between the two pKa values defines the isoelectric point (pI) of glycine, where the molecule carries no net charge. The experiment also demonstrated how the addition of a strong base shifts the curve to the right, requiring more base to reach equivalence, and how the shape of the curve changes when a weaker base is used. This titration protocol is a classic illustration of acid–base equilibria in amino acids and serves as a foundation for understanding protein charge behavior at different pH values.

## bp798-001 Which variable in the titration experiment is the independent variable?
Correct: A
Analogy: A 10‑year‑old sees the amount of water poured into a glass as the thing that changes first; the glass level (pH) follows. Official: In a titration, the researcher controls the volume of titrant added, making it the independent variable. Why wrong: Thinking pH or moles NaOH are independent ignores that the researcher deliberately varies the titrant volume to observe the response.

## bp798-002 If the researchers replaced NaOH with NH₄OH (a weaker base), how would the titration curve change?
Correct: A
Analogy: A 10‑year‑old thinks a weaker push will make the ball roll farther; the same idea applies here—using a weaker base means the same amount of proton removal takes longer. Official: A weaker base has a lower OH⁻ concentration, so more volume is needed to neutralize the same amount of acid, shifting the curve rightward. Why wrong: Believing the curve shifts left or stays the same ignores the reduced basic strength.

## bp798-003 Using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of glycine.
Correct: B
Analogy: A 10‑year‑old averages two numbers to find the middle; the same idea applies to pKa values. Official: pI = (pKa1 + pKa2)/2 = (2.34 + 9.60)/2 = 6.97. Why wrong: Choosing 5.97 or 7.97 misapplies the averaging or uses a single pKa.

## bp798-004 Which of the following units is incorrectly used in the table?
