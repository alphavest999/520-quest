Researchers conducted a randomized controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy of a novel cognitive-behavioral intervention (CBI) on reducing anxiety symptoms among adults with generalized anxiety disorder (GAD). Eighty participants were randomly assigned to either the CBI group (n=40) or a wait‑list control group (n=40). Baseline assessments revealed comparable anxiety scores across groups (mean 50.2, SD 5.1). Over a 12‑week intervention period, participants in the CBI group received weekly 60‑minute sessions delivered by trained therapists, while controls received no intervention. Post‑intervention anxiety scores were significantly lower in the CBI group (mean 60.5, SD 4.8) compared to controls (mean 52.0, SD 5.3), yielding a p‑value <0.001. The study also measured secondary outcomes, including depressive symptoms and functional impairment, which showed similar patterns of improvement. The authors noted limitations such as a relatively short follow‑up period and the absence of blinding. Despite these constraints, the findings suggest that CBI may be an effective treatment for GAD, warranting further research with larger samples and longer follow‑up.
